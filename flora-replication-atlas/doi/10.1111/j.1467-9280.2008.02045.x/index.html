The Value of Believing in Free Will

Kathleen D. Vohs, Jonathan W. Schooler

Psychological Science, 2008. DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02045.x

Has this study been replicated?

The atlas records 3 replications of this study. Recorded outcomes: 3 failed. The earliest on record is from 2015.

Replications

  • Many Labs 5: Testing Pre-Data-Collection Peer Review as an Intervention to Increase Replicability, Ebersole et al. (2020). Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science. Outcome recorded: failed. 95% CI for the meta-analytic replication includes 0 under both the RP:P and Revised Protocols (effect not significant in the same direction as the original under either). Outcome read from coding note: Table 3. View paper
  • Estimating the reproducibility of psychological science, Collaboration (2015). Science. Outcome recorded: failed. Coded as not replicated: Replicate = 0 (the project's binary success indicator combining significance and direction). Outcome read from coding note: data file (osf.io). View paper
  • Many Labs 5: Registered Replication of Vohs and Schooler (2008), Experiment 1, Buttrick et al. (2020). Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science. Outcome recorded: failed. We found that the revisions did not matter. Although the revised measure of belief in free will had better reliability than the original measure, an analysis of the data from the two protocols combined indicated that free-will beliefs were unchanged by the manipulations, d = 0.064, 95% confidence interval = [−0.087, 0.22], and in the focal test, there were no differences in cheating behavior between conditions, d = 0.076, 95% CI = [−0.082, 0.22]. Outcome read from the abstract. View paper
