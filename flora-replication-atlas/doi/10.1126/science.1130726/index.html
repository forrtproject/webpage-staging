Washing Away Your Sins: Threatened Morality and Physical Cleansing

Chen-Bo Zhong, Katie Liljenquist

Science, 2006. DOI 10.1126/science.1130726

Has this study been replicated?

The atlas records 5 replications of this study. Recorded outcomes: 4 failed, 1 successful. The earliest on record is from 2009.

Replications

  • Out, Damned Spot: Can the "Macbeth Effect" Be Replicated?, Earp et al. (2014). Basic and Applied Social Psychology. Outcome recorded: failed. Despite multiple good-faith efforts, however, we were unable to detect a "Macbeth Effect" in any of our experiments. Outcome read from the abstract. View paper
  • The Effect of Physical Cleaning on Threatened Morality in Individuals With Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder, Reuven et al. (2013). Clinical Psychological Science. Outcome recorded: successful. Replicating previous findings, physical cleaning reduced the willingness to help and relieved moral emotions. Outcome read from the abstract. View paper
  • Many Labs 2: Investigating Variation in Replicability Across Samples and Settings, Klein et al. (2018). Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science. Outcome recorded: failed. This result is not consistent with the hypothesis that copying an account of an unethical action increases the desirability of cleans- ing products compared with copying an account of an ethical action. Outcome read from p. 22. View paper
  • The Uncertain Universality of the Macbeth Effect with a Spanish Sample, Gámez et al. (2011). The Spanish journal of psychology. Outcome recorded: failed. We were unsuccessful in replicating the Zhong & Liljenquist ́s results that supported cleansing as a psychological mechanism for compensating guilty: results couldn't confirm an increased mental accessibility of cleansing-related concepts or even a greater desire for cleansing products, neither a greater likelihood of taking antiseptic wipes. Outcome read from the abstract. View paper
  • Is cleanliness next to godliness? Dispelling old wives' tales: Failure to replicate Zhong and Liljenquist (2006), Fayard et al. (2009). Journal of Articles in Support of the Null Hypothesis. Outcome recorded: failed. Despite larger samples and thus greater power to detect effects, both studies failed to replicate the original work of Zhong and Liljenquist (2006). Outcome read from the abstract.
